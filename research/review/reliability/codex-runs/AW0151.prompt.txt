# Claim-evaluability rubric (v0.1, 2026-10-14; under reliability test, not yet frozen)

Purpose: decide, for one paper, whether its primary claim can be checked from the paper itself.
The rater sees the abstract, every table and figure caption, and the tables as text. The rater
does not judge whether the claim is true.

## Step 1. Primary claim
The first statement in the abstract that asserts an advantage or a quantitative result for the
paper's own contribution. Paraphrase it in at most 25 words. If the abstract asserts none, the
claim type is `none`.

## Step 2. Claim type (exactly one)

| Code | Claim type | The quantity the claim is about |
|---|---|---|
| CT1 | Fewer denoising steps or forward passes at comparable quality | quality of method and baseline at equal forward passes |
| CT2 | Faster in wall-clock time, throughput or latency (caching, kernels, systems) | quality and measured time of method and baseline on the same hardware |
| CT3 | Better quality from an inference-time technique (remasking, corrector, unmasking order, guidance, constrained decoding) | quality with and without the technique at equal forward passes |
| CT4 | Better quality from a training or post-training change (objective, fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, distillation, architecture) | quality with and without the change at equal data and training budget |
| CT5 | Benefit of unifying tasks or modalities (synergy, joint training) | quality on one task with and without the other task in training |
| CT6 | Parity with or superiority over autoregressive models | quality of both at comparable size and a stated decoding budget |
| CT7 | Theoretical result (bound, equivalence, convergence) or a benchmark, dataset or analysis with no method advantage claimed | not scored |
| none | no advantage or quantitative claim in the abstract | not scored |

Tie-breaks: a method that changes inference only is CT1 if its abstract leads with steps or
forward passes, CT2 if it leads with time or throughput, CT3 if it leads with quality. A new model
that claims to match or beat autoregressive models is CT6 even if it also changes training. A new
unified model that claims the tasks help each other is CT5; one that only reports scores on both
kinds of task is CT4 or CT6 according to the comparison it leads with.

## Step 3. Minimum evaluability criterion (MEC)

The least a paper must report for the claimed quantity to be read off its own tables.

| Type | `met` | `partial` | `absent` |
|---|---|---|---|
| CT1 | the baseline sampler is reported at a step or forward-pass count no more than twice the method's, on the same task, model and metric | the baseline is reported at two or more budgets but none within twice the method's, or the matched comparison is on a different task than the headline | the baseline appears at one budget only |
| CT2 | quality is reported for the same configuration whose time is reported, the baseline is timed by the authors, and the hardware is named | two of the three | one or none |
| CT3 | a with-and-without comparison on the same model and task, with forward passes or steps reported for both arms | a with-and-without comparison where the cost of the arms is not reported | no with-and-without comparison |
| CT4 | a comparison against the same model trained without the change, with data or training steps stated for both arms | such a comparison without the budget of the arms, or against a different checkpoint of the same family | comparison only against other models |
| CT5 | the same backbone trained with and without the other task (or with shared against decoupled pathways), other settings stated equal | such an arm exists but other settings also change, or it is reported for one direction only | scores for both task kinds listed with no such arm |
| CT6 | an autoregressive baseline within a factor of two in parameters, evaluated by the authors under their own harness, with the diffusion model's steps or forward passes stated | a size-comparable autoregressive baseline whose numbers are copied from another paper, or the diffusion decoding budget is not stated | no size-comparable autoregressive baseline |
| CT7, none | code `na` | | |

A comparison shown only in a figure counts if the caption names the arms; record "figure" in the
evidence field. If the tables needed are cut off in the extract, choose the lower category and set
confidence to `low`.

## Step 4. Uncertainty (all empirical types)
`yes` if the headline comparison is reported with repeated runs (three or more seeds), a standard
deviation, a confidence interval or a significance test that the paper defines. An undefined
plus-minus is `no`.

## Output, one JSON object per paper
`{"record_id": "...", "primary_claim": "...", "claim_type": "CT1|CT2|CT3|CT4|CT5|CT6|CT7|none",
"mec": "met|partial|absent|na", "mec_evidence": "table or figure label, or 'none found'",
"uncertainty": "yes|no|na", "confidence": "high|low"}`

## Why these criteria and not others
Each criterion is the comparison that defines the claimed quantity: a speed-up at comparable
quality is a statement about two samplers at equal cost, so the baseline must be observed near
that cost; a benefit of unification is a statement about a model with and without the second task.
The criteria were written before any paper in the reliability sample was rated. They set a floor:
meeting one does not make a claim true, and a paper can meet it and still be confounded.

# Insertion Language Models: Sequence Generation with Arbitrary-Position Insertions

record AW0151 | arXiv:2505.05755 | first version 2025-05-09

**Abstract.** Autoregressive models (ARMs), which predict subsequent tokens one-by-one ``from left to right,'' have achieved significant success across a wide range of sequence generation tasks. However, they struggle to accurately represent sequences that require satisfying sophisticated constraints or whose sequential dependencies are better addressed by out-of-order generation. Masked Diffusion Models (MDMs) address some of these limitations, but the process of unmasking multiple tokens simultaneously in MDMs can introduce incoherences, and MDMs cannot handle arbitrary infilling constraints when the number of tokens to be filled in is not known in advance. In this work, we introduce Insertion Language Models (ILMs), which learn to insert tokens at arbitrary positions in a sequence -- that is, they select jointly both the position and the vocabulary element to be inserted. By inserting tokens one at a time, ILMs can represent strong dependencies between tokens, and their ability to generate sequences in arbitrary order allows them to accurately model sequences where token dependencies do not follow a left-to-right sequential structure. To train ILMs, we propose a tailored network parameterization and use a simple denoising objective. Our empirical evaluation demonstrates that ILMs outperform both ARMs and MDMs on common planning tasks. Furthermore, we show that ILMs outperform MDMs and perform on par with ARMs in an unconditional text generation task while offering greater flexibility than MDMs in arbitrary-length text infilling. The code is available at: https://dhruveshp.com/projects/ilm .
