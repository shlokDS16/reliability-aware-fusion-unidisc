## Claim-by-claim

1. **NEEDS-EVIDENCE.** A “required control” can be defined without circularity if rules are set before coding and tied to the claim’s estimand. But the examples mix different purposes: a step-matched baseline tests a speed claim; repeated runs quantify uncertainty; a task-removal arm tests whether a component contributes. Call these *minimum evaluability criteria* and justify each from the claim type. Otherwise “required” risks becoming a label assigned after seeing what papers report.

2. **NEEDS-EVIDENCE.** A smaller controlled effect is not automatically regression to the mean. It could reflect selective headline comparisons, but also different budgets, metrics, or estimands. To support (iii), the headline and controlled figures must measure the same outcome and differ only in the specified control. Even then, the result describes papers that included controls; it cannot establish what omitted controls would have shown.

3. **REJECT, as an organising conjecture in its current form.** Claim (i) is a falsifiable audit prediction. Claim (ii) is another empirical trend prediction, though its “does not rise” threshold needs specification. Claim (iii) is a distinct effect-size prediction. Bundling them under “control deficit” makes the central idea unclear, and the pilot supports an omission concern only for the sampled claim types—not the proposed below-half rate for every type or the time and publication-status predictions.

## Strongest counter-argument

The proposal’s strongest point is that the review should assess whether claims are checkable, rather than infer model properties from confounded benchmarks. Its weakness is presenting an appraisal framework as a conjecture about the literature before establishing that the criteria are principled and consistently applicable. The 15-paper pilot, especially with only two computable slack estimates, cannot establish the broad prevalence claims.

## Cheapest settling observation

Before expanding the audit, have two raters independently apply a preregistered rubric to a small, stratified sample. Include borderline cases and report agreement by claim type. If the raters cannot reliably identify the claim and its minimum control, “required control” is not yet a usable measure. Then estimate prevalence on the full corpus; treat the trend and effect-size predictions as separate analyses.

## POSITION

Use a systematic critical review organized around **claim evaluability**. Present the control-deficit idea as a pre-registered audit hypothesis, not yet as the review’s established organising conjecture. Keep (iii) separate and test it only for genuinely comparable within-paper contrasts.

## CONCESSIONS

The pilot gives a concrete reason to investigate missing controls, and the revised framing avoids treating sparse, selected speed-up comparisons as evidence that most acceleration is slack.

## OPEN DISAGREEMENTS

Whether minimum controls can be specified reliably across claim types, and whether their prevalence is below half or changes over time, remains unshown.

## WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND

A preregistered rubric with strong independent-rater agreement, followed by corpus-wide results supporting the prevalence and trend predictions, would make the control-deficit conjecture a credible organizing result.
