## POSITION

Paper 1 has a credible path to new knowledge, but the transcript does not establish that it has reached it. A PRISMA process and a large corpus strengthen coverage; they do not by themselves turn a catalogue into a contribution. The strongest candidate is a reproducible synthesis of *how reported DLM-versus-autoregressive comparisons are constructed and what conclusions those comparisons support*—if the extraction actually enables matched comparisons and the review reports limits as well as counts.

## CONCESSIONS

1. **ACCEPT, with a qualification.** A catalogue organized by taxonomy is unlikely to be enough when many surveys already cover the area. But the claim that readers cannot check a taxonomy is too broad: a precise coding scheme and paper-level evidence could make it auditable. The transcript does not show whether those materials exist.

2. **NEEDS-EVIDENCE.** A novel count can be useful, but “nobody had this number” is not sufficient for new knowledge. The result must be robust to the definition of a headline result, sampling, and extraction errors—and should matter for interpreting model claims. Within-paper contrast synthesis could be stronger, but only if comparisons are genuinely matched on budgets and evaluation conditions, rather than merely extracted as reported.

3. **ACCEPT.** Reporting the 11 partial fits and limits would make C1 more informative than a taxonomy that fits everything by construction. A checkable prediction could add explanatory value. But the proposed example—speed-ups that “must” accompany a value-rule change—needs a derivation and evidence. The transcript gives neither, and the claim should not be presented as a prediction until that test is specified.

4. **ACCEPT, with a qualification.** Model-by-model summaries would obscure the review’s analytical contribution. Opening sections with claims and counts is a useful discipline, but a count-led structure alone can still produce a generated catalogue. The synthesis needs to explain what the counts imply and how strong the underlying evidence is.

5. **REJECT as stated.** Whether EXP-003 belongs in a review depends on the review’s scope and the experiment’s role, not only on whether it changes a census conclusion. If it is an illustrative test of a gap the review identifies, a short, clearly separated section could support the argument. If its methods or results carry an independent claim, it risks making the paper harder to evaluate as a systematic review.

## OPEN DISAGREEMENTS

The strongest counter-argument to Claude’s main position is that the proposed census may measure *disclosure practices* rather than model behavior. A finding that papers omit or vary value rules could be important, but it does not establish that those rules explain reported gains or invalidate comparisons. Without a structured synthesis of the consequences, the paper risks replacing a model catalogue with a reporting-practice catalogue.

The cheapest observation to settle the census-artifact disagreement is a small, independently coded re-sample of the same papers, using a preregistered operational definition of “headline result” and “value rule,” then comparing classifications. That tests whether the striking count survives a plausible change in sampling and coding. It does not settle whether the census is important; that requires showing its implications for interpreting published comparisons.

## WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND

I would call the contribution new knowledge if the review presents auditable coding and a robust contrast synthesis that yields a conclusion readers could not get from the surveys—especially a defensible account of how often DLM advantages survive matched comparison conditions, and where the evidence is too weak to tell. I would downgrade it if the main results remain counts of papers, categories, and reporting omissions without showing what those patterns change in our understanding.
